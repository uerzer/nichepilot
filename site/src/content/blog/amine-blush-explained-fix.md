# Amine Blush: What It Is and How to Fix It

Amine blush is a waxy, oily, hazy film that appears on the surface of some cured resins — most commonly epoxy — and it makes a supposedly glossy piece look dull and sticky. It is not a mixing error. It is a chemical reaction between the hardener and moisture or carbon dioxide in the air during the early cure. It is also one of the most fixable resin defects, because the film sits on the surface and washes off.

## What amine blush looks like

A blushed surface looks like a light fog, a greasy streak, or a dull waxy layer that resists wiping. It often appears only at certain angles, and it can be mistaken for a cloudy finish or a thin residue. Touching it feels slightly oily or waxy rather than glassy. The coating is the giveaway that it is blush rather than a bubble or a scratch.

## Why it forms

Craft epoxy hardeners are often amines, and amines react with carbon dioxide (from the air you breathe) and with moisture to form carbamate salts. These compounds separate to the resin's surface during the cure and dry into the waxy film. The conditions that raise the risk are:

- High humidity (above about 60% RH) in the curing room.
- Carbon dioxide in the air — visible when people work nearby or in poorly ventilated spaces.
- Cold, damp conditions that slow cure and leave the surface reactive longer.
- Polyurethane resins, which blush especially aggressively.

The film forms during cure because the surface stays chemically active until the reaction completes. Once the resin is fully cured and demolded months later, the blush is whatever dried residue remains on top.

## How to clean amine blush off

Blush is water-soluble on many resins because it is a salt. Wash the cured surface with warm soapy water and a soft cloth or sponge, rinse thoroughly, and dry completely. Do not reach for solvents like acetone first, because they can soften the cured resin and streak the surface. If warm water does not fully lift the film, use isopropyl alcohol on a soft cloth and buff gently, then rinse.

After washing, the surface may still be too dull to call finished. Polish it with a fine buffing compound to restore gloss, or apply a thin fresh coat of resin over the sanded, cleaned surface.

## Prevention for your next pours

- Cure in low humidity — under about 55% relative humidity if possible.
- Ventilate the space so carbon dioxide does not build up.
- Keep the pour at the recommended temperature; a healthy cure finishes the surface reaction faster and blushes less.
- In damp months, pour smaller batches with better airflow, or place a dehumidifier in the room.

## When blush is not the problem

Some dull, hazy finishes are not blush: moisture entrapment and micro-bubbles also cause cloudiness, and a fingerprint or oily residue from a release agent can look similar. The test for blush is whether a warm soapy wash removes it. If water clears the haze, it was blush. If the milkiness remains after washing, the cloud is in the pour.

## Bottom Line

Amine blush is a waxy, hazy surface film formed when the amine hardener reacts with moisture and carbon dioxide during early cure. It is not a mixing defect, and it is fixable: wash the cured surface with warm soapy water, dry it, then polish or recoat. Prevent blush by curing in low humidity with good ventilation at the recommended temperature. When a haze washes off, you preserved a good piece instead of re-pouring.

## FAQ

**Q: What does amine blush look like?**
A: A waxy, oily haze on the cured resin surface — dull, slightly greasy, and often only visible at glancing angles. It resembles a fog rather than a bubble or scratch.

**Q: How do I remove amine blush?**
A: Wash the cured surface with warm soapy water and a soft cloth, rinse, and dry. If needed, buff with isopropyl alcohol, then polish. Skip heavy solvents first to avoid softening the resin.

**Q: Why does my resin keep blushing?**
A: High humidity, carbon dioxide in the air, and cool damp curing conditions all promote blush. Cure in a dry, ventilated space at the recommended temperature.

**Q: Do all resins blush?**
A: No. Blush is most common in amine-based epoxy hardeners and in polyurethane systems. Many modern craft epoxies are formulated to reduce it but can still blush in damp conditions.

**Q: Is blush dangerous?**
A: On the finished surface, no — it is a cosmetic residue. Thе risk is that you mistake it for a defective pour and re-pour unnecessarily, or fail to wash off the film before finishing.

**Q: Can I just leave the blush on?**
A: You can, but the finish will stay dull and slightly waxy, and a later topcoat may not adhere well. Washing the blush off is quick and improves adhesion.
