# Why Dried Flowers Turn Brown in Resin and How to Prevent It

You embedded a vivid flower, it looked perfect through the pour, and within a week it had browned. This is the most common botanical-resin letdown, and it is rarely a resin problem — it is a moisture and oxidation problem the flower carried into the pour. Understanding why browning happens tells you exactly how to prevent it.

## The cause: moisture and the resin's exotherm

Fresh or imperfectly dried flowers contain water, and water is the enemy of clear resin. When the resin's exothermic reaction heats the pour — even gently — it drives moisture out of the botanical. That moisture reacts with the resin to form cloudy haze, but it also speeds up the oxidation that turns the flower's organic pigments brown. The greener and fresher the flower, the more moisture it releases, and the faster it browns.

Beyond moisture, oxidation itself darkens floral pigment over time, the same way cut flowers fade and darken. The sealed resin slows air exchange but does not stop the oxidation, so even a fully dry flower will eventually deepen. Your job is to make the flower as dry and protected as possible so the piece stays bright for months rather than days.

## Dry thoroughly before you pour

The single most effective prevention is bone-dry botanicals. Two reliable methods:

### Silica gel drying
Bury flowers completely in silica gel desiccant in a sealed container for 3 to 7 days. Silica removes moisture deeply and evenly, preserving color better than air drying. Check that the crystals still trap moisture (they turn pink when saturated) and replace them if needed.

### Press drying
Press flowers flat between absorbent paper inside a heavy book or flower press for 2 to 3 weeks. The long duration gives moisture time to leave. Pressed flowers come out flat, best for thin pieces.

Whichever you use, the flower must be uniform and papery — no give, no dampness. Test by pressing carefully; a fully dry petal is crisp, not rubbery.

## Seal the dry flower before embedding

Even fully dried flowers hold trace moisture. Brushing a thin coat of resin onto the dried flower and letting it cure isolates the botanical so residual moisture cannot react with the surrounding pour. This barrier dramatically slows both the cloudy halo and the browning. Seal the flower, cure the sealer, then embed in the main pour.

## Choose color-stable blooms

Some colors survive resin better than others. Deep, saturated pigments — burgundies, deep purples, greens, and strong yellows — hold up best. Delicate whites, very pale pinks, and soft pastels turn translucent or beige quickly because their light pigmentation shows the natural darkening earliest. Pick vivid, mature blooms picked at their peak and dry them promptly after picking.

## Accept and plan for natural aging

All preserved botanicals eventually shift color. In a resin piece, a well-dried, sealed flower keeps its color for many months to years, then gradually deepens. Design with that in mind: choose blooms whose natural aging remains attractive, seal thoroughly, and store finished pieces out of direct sun, which accelerates oxidation.

## Bottom Line

Flowers brown in resin chiefly because they carry moisture that the resin's heat drives out, speeding oxidation of the flower's pigments. Prevent it by drying blooms bone-dry with silica gel (or pressing fully), sealing the dried flower in a thin resin coat before embedding, picking color-stable saturated blooms, and keeping finished pieces out of direct sunlight. The drier and better-sealed the flower, the longer it stays vivid.

## FAQ

**Q: Why did my flower turn brown in the resin?**
A: The flower carried moisture into the pour. The resin's exothermic heat drove that moisture out, and it sped up oxidation of the flower's pigments, browning them.

**Q: How do I keep flowers from turning brown in resin?**
A: Dry them completely (silica gel for 3–7 days), seal the dried flower in a thin resin coat before embedding, and store the finished piece out of direct sunlight.

**Q: Can fresh flowers go into resin without drying?**
A: No. Fresh flowers brown and cloud the pour. Every botanical must be bone-dry and sealed before embedding.

**Q: Do some flowers stay colorful in resin better than others?**
A: Yes. Deep, saturated blooms (burgundy, purple, green, strong yellow) hold color best. Pale pinks and whites turn beige or translucent quickly.

**Q: How long does a sealed flower stay colorful in resin?**
A: A well-dried, sealed flower typically stays vivid for many months to years, then gradually deepens. Direct sun accelerates the darkening.

**Q: Does air drying or silica drying preserve flower color better?**
A: Silica gel preserves color better because it removes moisture faster and more evenly, limiting oxidation during drying.
