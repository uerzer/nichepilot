Best Resin Art Kit for Beginners: What to Buy

Compare beginner resin art kits by project, included tools, and missing supplies, with manufacturer sources and a checklist to avoid buying twice.

Sep 13, 2026 · Updated Sep 17, 2026

Choose a kit for one specific project, not for the number of accessories in the photograph. This guide compares two documented options and shows what to check before paying.

Research, not a hands-on review: this comparison uses manufacturer listings checked September 17, 2026; we have not tested these kits. The product links below are ordinary, non-affiliate links and do not earn Poured a commission.

What should you check before adding a kit to your cart?

Open the instructions as well as the product description. Keep the listing beside this checklist and mark each item confirmed, missing, or unclear:

  1. Project compatibility. Does the manufacturer describe the use you intend: coating, casting in a mold, or another application? Do not treat an attractive finished-project photo as a technical specification.
  2. Package contents. Is the stated quantity the combined resin and hardener, or only one component? Are the colors and molds shown actually included with your selected variant?
  3. Measurement method. Record the manufacturer’s ratio and whether it is measured by volume or weight. Do not copy a ratio from a different brand or assume the two methods are interchangeable.
  4. Pour and batch limits. Find the permitted layer depth and batch size in the current instructions. If you cannot find a limit for your planned pour, ask the manufacturer before purchasing.
  5. Working conditions. Find the recommended temperature, working time, demolding guidance, and full-cure guidance for that particular system.
  6. Safety information. Locate the safety data sheets for the components and check whether you can meet the required handling precautions in your workspace.
  7. Delivered cost. Compare the total including the missing supplies and delivery, rather than the product price alone.

Do not interpret an unclear answer as permission to improvise. A seller who cannot supply the relevant instructions is not a useful starting point for a first project. For a broader inventory, use the resin art supplies list, but let the specific product documentation decide compatibility and safety requirements.

Should a beginner always buy 1:1 epoxy?

Use the ratio specified for the product, not a generic “beginner ratio.” A convenient measuring routine is useful, but it does not tell you whether a formula suits a mold, a coating, or a deeper casting. Choose the application first and learn the measurement method second.

Keep a written preparation card with the exact product name, component names, ratio, measurement method, and instructions location. This is especially useful if you own more than one resin system: a familiar bottle shape is not evidence that the instructions match. Leave timing fields blank until you have read the current manufacturer directions.

Before buying UV resin instead, check the product’s curing equipment and application requirements. Do not assume a lamp from another craft will work, or that a UV product can replace a two-part epoxy in a tutorial without other changes. Start with one documented system rather than purchasing several types to “see what happens.”

Which safety supplies belong in the budget?

CDC/NIOSH advises following the label or safety data sheet, increasing ventilation, and choosing protective equipment appropriate to the resin; the correct glove material matters.[5] Its guidance also says that smelling or not smelling a chemical is not a reliable indication of safety.[5] A low-odor claim is therefore not a reason to skip checking the safety documentation.

There is no “under 200 ml means no respirator needed” rule in this guide. Do not select respiratory protection from batch volume alone. Use the product’s safety instructions and appropriate professional guidance for your working conditions; if those requirements are unclear or cannot be met, postpone the pour rather than guessing.

CDC/NIOSH recommends eye protection where chemicals could splash into the eyes.[5] It also advises people trying to conceive, pregnant, or breastfeeding to avoid mixing epoxies and resins themselves.[5] Review that guidance and discuss relevant concerns with a qualified healthcare professional.

Budget for the protection and workspace your actual product requires before adding decorative extras. A few disposable gloves in a bundle do not establish that the whole workspace is ready. Do not treat an included torch as an instruction to use open flame: read both the resin and tool instructions, and do not improvise with a lighter.

How much should you spend?

Avoid a universal budget ceiling. We link to current seller prices rather than maintaining a price ranking that could be stale by the time you read it. A higher price does not prove better resin, and a low price alone does not prove poor quality.

Make a simple shopping sheet with four rows: the kit, missing project supplies, required protection, and delivery or taxes. Enter only prices you can currently verify for the variant and destination you will actually buy. If an item has no confirmed price, leave it blank rather than filling the gap with an estimate presented as fact.

Now compare that total with buying the components separately. Keep the project constant: the same mold or surface, the same desired colors, and enough compatible material for the intended pour. Reject comparisons that make one basket look cheaper by quietly omitting protection or using a different resin quantity.

For planning beyond the first purchase, our first-year cost breakdown provides categories to think about, not a substitute for today’s quotes. Start with a modest project and resist buying bulk material before you know the storage requirements and whether you want to continue the hobby.

Which buying mistakes are easiest to avoid?

Buying an accessory count instead of a project. A hundred glitter packets do not answer whether the kit includes the mold, compatible resin, measuring equipment, and instructions you need. Compare the essential inventory before the decorative extras.

Using an older tutorial without checking the supplied formula. Read the product name on the bottles and match it to the current instructions. Our cure-times guide explains the distinction between cure stages, but its general discussion does not override the timing for your product.

Confusing demolding with readiness for use. Record both milestones when the manufacturer supplies them. Do not schedule a gift or a sale around the earliest time a piece might come out of a mold.

Buying before choosing a workspace. The right next purchase may be none. If you cannot meet the handling and ventilation requirements, change the workspace or choose a different activity rather than trying to compensate with a larger kit.

Frequently asked questions

Is either kit independently tested by Poured?

No. This is a manufacturer-documentation comparison. We have not bought, mixed, cured, or measured these products, and we do not claim personal results.

Which should I compare first for colored coasters?

Start with the TotalBoat coaster kit because its listing explicitly includes silicone molds and colorants.[7] Still check the current contents, instructions, and your complete delivered cost before deciding.

Does the ArtResin starter pack include everything for coasters?

Its listed contents do not include molds or pigments.[3] Treat it as a coating-focused candidate and build a separate missing-supplies list if you want to use it for another application supported by its instructions.

Can I rely on the kit’s included gloves for every session?

Do not assume that. Check the product-specific requirements and replacement supply you need; CDC/NIOSH notes that gloves must be the right material to protect you.[5] Included accessories are not a complete safety assessment.

Compare the TotalBoat kit for molded coasters and the ArtResin starter pack for coating an artwork, using current contents rather than marketing claims. Read the instructions and safety information before paying, then compare complete delivered baskets.
