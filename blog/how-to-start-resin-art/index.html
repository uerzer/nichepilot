How to Start Resin Art: A Complete Beginner Guide

Everything you need to start resin art from zero — supplies, workspace setup, your first pour, and curing basics.

Sep 14, 2026

Resin art looks intimidating, but the learning curve is short. Most people go from unboxing to a decent set of coasters in a single weekend. Here’s the full path from zero to first finished piece.

Step 1: Set Up Your Workspace

You need a flat, level surface covered in a silicone mat or plastic sheeting — resin drips are forever. Room temperature matters more than anything: aim for 20–25°C. Cold rooms cause microbubbles and tacky cures, the two most common beginner complaints, and both trace back to temperature.

Ventilation: open a window and run a fan. You don’t need a fume hood for small craft pours, but don’t work in a sealed box either.

Step 2: Gather the Minimum Kit

  • Two-part epoxy resin, 1:1 ratio (500ml is plenty to start)
  • Silicone coaster molds
  • Mica powders or alcohol inks (pick 3 colors you actually like together)
  • Silicone mixing cups, stir sticks, nitrile gloves
  • Long-neck lighter or heat gun for bubbles

That’s it. Skip the turners, the pressure pots, the vacuum chambers — those solve problems you don’t have yet.

Step 3: Your First Pour

  1. Put on gloves. Put on gloves. (People skip this once and learn.)
  2. Pour equal parts resin and hardener into a marked cup.
  3. Stir slowly for 3 minutes, scraping the sides and bottom. Slow matters — whipping adds bubbles.
  4. Divide into smaller cups and add pigment. A little goes far; you can always add more.
  5. Pour into the mold. Start in the center and let it flow outward.
  6. Wait 5 minutes, then pass the lighter quickly 10–15cm above the surface to pop bubbles. Don’t hold it in one spot.
  7. Cover with a cardboard box to keep dust off. Walk away for 24 hours.

Step 4: Demold and Finish

After 24 hours (48 for thick pieces), flex the silicone mold and pop the piece out. Edges may be sharp — wet-sand with 400 then 800 grit sandpaper, or dome a thin clear topcoat over the surface for a glass finish.

What to Make Next

Once coasters click, the natural progression is: trinket trays, then jewelry pendants (switch to UV resin for these), then serving boards. Each teaches one new skill — layering, inclusions, edge finishing — without a big jump in difficulty.

Bottom Line

Workspace, kit, pour, cure, demold. Five steps, one weekend, and you’ll know whether this hobby is for you. Total startup cost: under $60.
