Embedding Flowers in Resin: Complete Guide

How to embed fresh or dried flowers in resin: pressing, drying, and sealing methods, and how to prevent browning, cloudy rings, and air bubbles around botanicals

Embedding Flowers in Resin: Complete Guide

Real botanicals give resin pieces an organic depth that pigments cannot, but flowers also bring their own failure modes: browning, trapped air, and a cloudy ring where moisture reacts with the resin. The flower must be completely dry and sealed before it goes in, or it will rot, discolor, and release gas that ruins the pour. This guide covers drying methods, the sealing step most beginners skip, and how to lay botanicals for a clean result.

Why flowers must be bone-dry first

Fresh flowers contain up to 80% water, and that water is incompatible with resin. When a not-fully-dried flower is embedded, the moisture escapes as steam during the exotherm, producing bubbles, cloudiness, and a rotting specimen that browns and decays. The water also prevents the resin from bonding to the botanical, leaving a cloudy halo. Drying is not optional — every gram of moisture must be gone before the pour.

Drying methods compared

Air drying / pressing

Lay flowers flat between sheets of absorbent paper inside a heavy book or a flower press, and leave them for 2 to 3 weeks. This produces flat, pressed botanicals that are ideal for thin resin pieces like coasters and bookmarks. The result is flat, so it does not suit three-dimensional embedment.

Silica gel drying

Bury flowers completely in silica gel desiccant crystals in an airtight container and wait 3 to 7 days. Silica gas absorbs moisture without flattening the flower, preserving its shape for deep pours and jewelry. The crystals turn pink when saturated and can be dried back in an oven. This is the best method for three-dimensional botanicals.

Microwave drying

Microwave flower presses dry thin botanicals in 1 to 3 minutes. Speedy, but the result is fragile and can scorch delicate petals. Useful for a quick press, less reliable for color retention.

Cold resin-dry artifacts

Some crafters dry small flowers in a small amount of warmed resin so they cure within a hardened bead. This is effective for very small pieces but does not displace all moisture as reliably as silica.

Color retention

Silica-dried botanicals hold color better than air-dried ones because moisture leaves faster, limiting oxidation and browning. Delicate white flowers often turn translucent or beige regardless of method. For the brightest result, dry as soon as possible after picking when the flower is at its most vivid, and consider that some colors (deep blues, purples) survive drying and embedding better than very pale pastels.

The sealing step that prevents the halo

Even dried flowers hold microscopic moisture that resin reacts with, forming a gray or cloudy ring around the botanical. The fix is to seal the dried flower in a thin layer of resin — or a light spray of clear UV-stable sealer — and let it cure before the main embedment. This barrier isolery prevents moisture and air from reaching the resin interface. For flat pressed botanicals, brush a thin coat of resin onto the flower, cure it, then pour the surrounding layer.

Laying the flower for a bubble-free pour

Place the sealed flower into a thin base layer of resin already poured in the mold, pressing it down to release trapped air. Then pour a second layer to enclose it. Pour slowly down a stick to let air escape, and torch the surface lightly to pop bubbles. Keep the flower oriented so no air pockets sit under its petals or leaves — a small tool, like a pin or angled stick, helps push the botanical down into the base coat.

Propping to prevent floating

Flowers are lighter than resin and want to float. The base-coat-then-embed method holds the flower’s position while the base cures. For deep pours, anchors: set the flower on a bed of cured resin, then pour additional resin in measured layers, each allowed to thicken before the next, so the botanical stays where you placed it rather than drifting to the top.

Bottom Line

Embedding flowers in resin works when the botanical is fully dry and sealed before the pour. Dry with silica gel for shape, or press for flat pieces, then seal the flower in a thin resin layer to stop the cloudy halo. Lay the sealed flower into a base coat, pour enclosing layers slowly, and torch the surface. Moisture is the enemy — the drier and better-sealed the flower, the cleaner the finished crystal.

FAQ

Q: Do I need to dry flowers before putting them in resin? A: Yes, completely. Fresh flowers contain water that creates bubbles, cloudiness, and browning. Every specimen must be bone-dry — silica-dried or pressed — before embedding.

Q: How do I dry flowers for resin without a press? A: Bury them completely in silica gel desiccant in a sealed container for 3–7 days. This preserves shape and color better than air drying for three-dimensional pieces.

Q: Why does my embedded flower have a cloudy ring around it? A: That halo comes from residual moisture or air at the flower-resin interface. Seal the dried flower in a thin resin coat first, and lay it into a base layer pressing out air, before the enclosing pour.

Q: Can I use fresh flowers straight from the garden? A: Not directly. Dry them fully first. Fresh botanical moisture causes bubbles, cloudiness, and rot.

Q: Why does the flower keep floating in the resin? A: Flowers are less dense than resin. Set the flower in a cured base layer, then pour enclosing layers slowly so it stays in place.

Q: Do flowers turn brown in resin? A: Some do. Browning comes from residual moisture and oxidation. Silica drying preserves color best, and darker, saturated flowers survive the process better than pale pastels.
