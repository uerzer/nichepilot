Resin Art Glossary: 40 Terms Every Beginner Needs

Resin craft has a dense vocabulary, and the terms get thrown around on packaging, datasheets, and tutorials as if everyone already knows them. This glossary defines the 40 terms you will actually hit in your first year, in plain language, so a spec sheet reads like instructions instead of a code.

The chemistry and cure terms

Resin. The liquid polymer component that, when mixed with a hardener, cures into a solid. Craft resin is usually epoxy.

Hardener. The second liquid component. Mixing resin and hardener in the stated ratio starts the curing reaction. It is sometimes called the “catalyst” or “curing agent.”

Mix ratio. The proportion of resin to hardener, by volume or weight, that must be exact. Most craft epoxies are 2:1 or 1:1 by volume. Getting this wrong causes sticky, soft, or cloudy results.

Pot life (working time). How long the mixed resin stays liquid and workable after mixing before it starts to thicken and gel. A longer pot life gives you more time to pour and manipulate.

Exotherm. The heat the mixing and curing reaction generates. A large, thick pour produces more heat; excessive exotherm can cause cracking, bubbles, or even a smoky burn in extreme cases.

Cure time. The period from mixing until the resin reaches its full hardness. Distinct from pot life: pot life is how long you can work; cure time is how long until it is solid.

Full cure / Demold time. The time after which the resin is hard enough to demold (leave the mold) — usually 24–72 hours depending on thickness and temperature.

Viscosity. How thick or runny the liquid is. High viscosity resin is honey-like and traps bubbles more; low viscosity flows readily and is easier to de-gas.

Cross-linking. The chemical reaction that bonds resin molecules into a rigid 3D network as it cures. Incomplete cross-linking leaves soft or tacky spots.

Flash cure. A fast, controlled application of heat to pop surface bubbles soon after pouring. It is not the same as oven-curing.

The materials terms

Epoxy resin. The common craft resin, made from a resin and a hardener that cure by exothermic reaction. Yellow-tolerant and versatile.

UV resin. A resin that cures with ultraviolet light rather than a two-part mix. Cures in minutes under a UV lamp, used for small jewelry items.

Polyurethane resin. A harder, tougher resin that can be preferable for certain applications but cures faster and is more moisture-sensitive.

Deep pour resin. A low-exotherm formulation rated to cure in pours of 1 inch or more in one layer, rather than the 1/4–1/2 inch limit of standard craft epoxy.

Mica powder. A fine, shimmery mineral pigment mixed into resin for metallic and pearlescent color.

Alcohol ink. A concentrated liquid dye used to add transparent or marbled color to resin.

Silicone mold. A flexible mold that releases cured resin easily. The standard mold material for resin shapes.

Mold release. A spray or wipe that helps cured resin separate from a mold, useful for complex or multi-part molds.

Primer / Sealant. A coat applied to porous surfaces (wood, cardboard) so resin does not soak in unevenly.

Base / Countertop epoxy. High-gloss, self-leveling epoxy meant to coat surfaces in thick pours; not always safe for small pieces.

The technique terms

Dirty pour. Loading several colors into one cup and pouring them together so they produce ribbons of layered color.

Flip cup. Inverting a cup loaded with layered colors onto the canvas, letting the resin flow outward.

Puddle pour. Pouring colors into separate puddles on the canvas and tilting to move them.

Cells. The lacy, organic circular patterns that form when silicone-based medium, mixed into resin colors, reacts with the resin during a pour.

Swirl. A technique (or a tool) that creates circular patterns by pulling one color through another.

Geode art. A piece styled to look like the interior of a geode — layered crystal clusters, metallic veins, and organic edges.

River table. A table with a channel of colored, often blue, resin running through the center like a river.

Domino / Doming. A thick, bubble-free coat that self-levels into a smooth shiny finish on a small piece.

Deep pour technique. Pouring in thick layers, often over inclusions, using a low-exotherm resin designed to go deep.

Embedding. Setting objects — flowers, photos, glitter, insects — inside a resin pour to seal them.

The recipe and finish terms

Mechanical properties / Shore hardness. A scale (Shore D for hard, Shore A for soft) that rates how hard the cured resin is. Higher Shore D means a stiffer, more scratch-resistant surface.

Sandability. How well cured resin takes sandpaper without clogging or gumming. Properly cured epoxy sands cleanly.

Barrier coat / Flood coat. A thin resin layer applied over an existing surface or previous coat to protect or add gloss.

Self-leveling. A resin property where the wet surface flows flat by itself, giving a smooth even finish without sanding.

Yellowing. The amber discoloration that occurs as cured resin oxidizes under UV light and heat.

Amine blush. A waxy, oily film that forms on the surface of some epoxies during cure, caused by moisture reacting with the hardener. It appears as a haze.

Oven-cure. Baking a poured piece in an oven to accelerate cure; usually causes yellowing and is not recommended.

Pot life extension. Techniques — cooling ingredients, smaller batches, slower mixing — that lengthen working time.

Bottom Line

Learn these 40 terms and the brand datasheets stop being confusing. The most important to internalize early are mix ratio, pot life, cure time, exotherm, and deep pour — these five govern most of your successes and most of your failures. Keep a glossary open for the first several projects, and the vocabulary will become second nature.

FAQ

Q: What is the difference between pot life and cure time? A: Pot life is how long the mixed resin stays workable and liquid after mixing. Cure time is how long until it reaches full hardness. Pot life is minutes; cure time is hours to days.

Q: What does exotherm mean in resin? A: Exotherm is the heat generated by the curing reaction. Large or thick pours produce more heat; excessive exotherm causes bubbles, cracking, or a smoky burn.

Q: What is a deep pour resin? A: A low-exotherm epoxy rated to cure in pours of 1 inch or more in a single layer, unlike standard craft epoxy limited to about 1/4–1/2 inch per pour.

Q: What causes amine blush? A: Moisture reacting with the hardener during cure forms a waxy, hazy film called amine blush. It appears especially in humid, cold, or poorly ventilated cures.

Q: Is UV resin the same as epoxy? A: No. UV resin cures with ultraviolet light in minutes and suits small pieces; two-part epoxy cures by chemical reaction over hours and is better for larger or thicker work.

Q: What does it mean for resin to be self-leveling? A: The wet resin flows flat on its own and settles into a smooth, even surface, so you do not need to spread or sand it level.
